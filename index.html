About the AUV

An autonomous underwater vehicle built around the team's own design. It surveys an area underwater, detects objects, assesses whether they may be hazardous, explains each decision and tells the operator which targets to inspect first.

The problem

Coastal and offshore waters hold marine life, natural features and man-made objects side by side: shipwrecks, debris, lost equipment and, in some areas, old ordnance. Some of these are hazards to navigation or to people working in the water; most are not.

Different sensing methods provide different types of information about underwater targets. Acoustic sensing can remain useful in poor visibility, while target interpretation still requires analysis of multiple characteristics. No single sensing source answers the question “what is this object, and is it a hazard?” on its own.

What the system does

The AUV concept surveys an area in parallel lines and combines acoustic and onboard sensor data to detect underwater targets. Each target is analysed, classified and given a risk level, a risk type and a decision with an explanation. When the evidence is not enough, the system reports the object as unknown and asks for verification instead of forcing a class.

Targets are marked and reported to the operator at the surface.

Operating environment

Target operating depth for the concept: 1–30 metres below the water surface. This is a conceptual range; the physical vehicle has not been tested or certified across it.

How it works

From the water to the operator: the AUV senses its surroundings, detects targets, classifies them, assesses their potential risk, explains each decision and recommends an action.

  1. Underwater Environment

    The survey area, 1–30 m below the surface (concept).

  2. Sensor / Acoustic Data

    Multibeam echosounder data, water-column acoustic data and onboard sensor data.

  3. Target Detection

    Candidate objects are found in the sensor data and given a target ID and a detection time.

  4. Target Feature Analysis

    Shape, geometry, structure, size category, movement and difference from the background are examined.

  5. Object Classification

    Marine life, man-made structure or potential hazardous object — or unknown when the evidence is not enough.

  6. Risk Assessment

    Non-hazardous, potential hazard (or high potential hazard), or unknown and requiring verification.

  7. Risk Type Identification

    For example navigation / obstruction risk or potential explosive hazard.

  8. Explainable Decision

    A decision together with the evidence and the rule that led to it.

  9. Target Marking

    The target is marked at its position on the survey map.

  10. Recommended Action

    Continue the survey, inspect the target, or verify it.

  11. Local Data Storage

    Target records and raw sensor data are stored on board.

  12. Data Transfer / Operator System

    Priority target records reach the operator at the surface.

Classification, risk and decision

Each target is analysed from its shape, geometry, structure, size category, movement where applicable, acoustic characteristics and difference from the surrounding background. The classification, the risk assessment and the recommended action are kept as three separate results.

Non-Hazardous

Marine life, and objects with no immediate hazard. Decision: continue survey / log observation.

Potential Hazard

Wrecks, obstructions and other man-made structures. Decision: requires inspection. A potential mine is a high potential hazard; it always requires verification and is never reported as confirmed.

Unknown — Requires Verification

Objects without enough evidence for a reliable class. The system says “unknown” instead of guessing. Decision: requires verification.

Potential risk is expressed as a priority for the operator to review; the system does not determine danger with certainty. The decision engine in this prototype is rule-based and explainable. Trained recognition models are future development and are not part of it.

Verification and background comparison

Multi-pass verification Conceptual

  1. First pass
  2. Target detected
  3. Additional pass, from another angle
  4. Evidence comparison
  5. Decision update

A suspicious target is passed again from the neighbouring survey line, so it is seen from another angle. The two observations are compared before the decision is updated. In the demo this appears as a conceptual multi-angle consensus; no trained model is involved.

Environmental / acoustic background Conceptual

A detected return is compared with the surrounding seafloor and water column. A return that stands out from its background is stronger evidence that an object is present. In the demo this comparison is a simulated evidence item; no trained anomaly-detection model is claimed.

Data transfer Conceptual

Underwater links carry little data, so the concept keeps large raw sensor data on board and sends compact target records first.

AUV design
